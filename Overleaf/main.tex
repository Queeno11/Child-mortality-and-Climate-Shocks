\title{The Unequal Burden of a Changing Climate: Extreme Weather Shocks and Infant Mortality}
\author{}
\date{\today}
\begin{document}
\maketitle


\begin{abstract}
This paper examines the causal impact of climate variability on infant mortality across sixty low- and middle-income countries between 1998 and 2021. Combining geo-referenced birth histories from the Demographic and Health Surveys (DHS) with high-resolution ERA5 climate data, we estimate a discrete-time hazard model with cell--month fixed effects and cell-specific linear trends to identify the effects of temperature and precipitation anomalies during gestation and early childhood on survival. We find that temperature anomalies substantially increase infant mortality, with gestational exposure producing the largest and most persistent effects, risks remain elevated well into the second year of life, whereas postnatal shocks are smaller and short-lived. Mortality responds primarily to sustained departures from normal conditions rather than isolated extreme events, underscoring the cumulative nature of climate stress. Precipitation tells a different story: monthly rainfall anomalies have little detectable effect, but sustained wet conditions over many months do raise mortality, consistent with slower-moving contamination and disease channels. Translating these estimates into population terms, temperature variability caused roughly 44 million additional infant deaths across DHS countries between 1992 and 2022, with the burden concentrated in tropical regions of Sub-Saharan Africa and South Asia. Impacts are amplified among households lacking basic infrastructure — particularly electricity and piped water — and the effects of in-utero exposure persist even among better-served households, pointing to the limits of adaptation when the critical damage occurs before birth. Taken together, the results reveal the cumulative and deeply unequal burden of climate variability on infant survival, and underscore the urgency of investments that protect maternal health during pregnancy and strengthen adaptive capacity among the most vulnerable populations.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction} \label{sec:introduction}

Child survival remains one of the most pressing challenges in global development. Despite remarkable progress over recent decades, 4.9 million children under five died in 2024 alone, with over four in five of those deaths concentrated in sub-Saharan Africa and Southern Asia \parencite{UNIGME2025}. Achieving the Sustainable Development Goal of ending preventable child deaths by 2030 will require confronting an increasingly prominent threat: climate change. Rising temperatures, more frequent droughts, and intensifying rainfall extremes are already disrupting the agricultural systems, water supplies, and health infrastructure that low- and middle-income country (LMIC) populations depend on \parencite{watts2018lancet}. The question of how climate variability affects child survival, and who bears the greatest burden, has therefore become central to both global health and development policy.

Children in LMICs are disproportionately exposed to climate shocks and disproportionately ill-equipped to cope with them. Limited access to healthcare, poor household infrastructure, and reliance on climate-sensitive livelihoods amplify the consequences of weather extremes \parencite{UNICEF2015}. Within this vulnerable population, the earliest stages of life stand out as periods of exceptional biological sensitivity. Gestation and the first year of life are critical developmental windows during which environmental stressors can have severe and lasting consequences for health and survival \parencite{adger2006fair}.\footnote{In this paper, we define climate shocks as standardized deviations from long-term historical averages, capturing unexpected departures from what households and communities have come to expect for a given month and location.} Understanding how climate variability operates through these windows and whether its effects compound over time, is essential for designing effective protective policies.

A growing body of research has begun to quantify these relationships. At the aggregate level, \textcite{burke2025} provide the most comprehensive cross-country assessment of temperature-related mortality to date, estimating that non-optimal temperatures account for 5--12 percent of all global deaths, with cold dominating heat-related mortality in temperate climates. However, their analysis focuses primarily on adult and elderly populations in higher-income settings. Recent microdata evidence fills part of this gap: \textcite{wang2025} show that prenatal exposure to extreme heat markedly increases neonatal mortality in sub-Saharan Africa, particularly among rural and electricity-poor households, and \textcite{dimitrova2024} provide the first formal attribution of neonatal deaths to anthropogenic climate change, estimating that 4.3 percent of neonatal mortality across 29 LMICs is linked to non-optimal temperatures. These contributions establish that climate variability kills infants, that the prenatal period is especially vulnerable, and that household infrastructure shapes exposure. Yet they leave three critical questions unanswered. First, how do climate effects evolve beyond the neonatal period, and do in-utero shocks leave a lasting imprint on survival throughout infancy? Second, do effects stem from acute extreme events or from sustained departures from normal conditions and does this distinction matter for the magnitude of estimated impacts? Third, how do temperature and precipitation compare as mortality drivers, and through what channels do they operate?

This paper addresses these questions using a global analysis of climate variability and infant mortality. We combine geo-referenced birth histories from the Demographic and Health Surveys (DHS) across sixty low- and middle-income countries with high-resolution ERA5 climate data, estimating a discrete-time hazard model with cell--month fixed effects and cell-specific linear trends to identify the causal effects of temperature and precipitation anomalies on survival from gestation through the first two years of life. Our central finding is that climate variability imposes a large, cumulative, and deeply unequal mortality burden on infants in the developing world. Temperature anomalies substantially raise mortality, particularly when exposure occurs in utero, with effects that persist well into the second year of life. This 
mortality risk is driven by sustained departures from normal conditions rather than isolated extreme events. Precipitation tells a different story: monthly rainfall anomalies have little immediate effect, but sustained wet conditions over many months do raise mortality, consistent with slower-moving contamination and disease channels. Translating these estimates into population terms, temperature variability caused roughly 44 million additional infant deaths across DHS countries between 1992 and 2022, with the burden concentrated in tropical Sub-Saharan Africa and South Asia — geographic patterns that closely mirror the \parencite{UNIGME2025} assessment of where the global child mortality burden falls today and where climate shocks pose the greatest compounding threat.

We make four contributions to this literature. First, we document the timing and persistence of temperature effects across early-life windows: gestational exposure produces the largest and most persistent mortality increases, with in-utero shocks raising risks throughout the first year of life even when postnatal conditions are normal. Second, we show that mortality responds to sustained anomalies rather than isolated extreme days, establishing the cumulative nature of climate stress as a central empirical regularity. Third, we contrast temperature and precipitation as mortality drivers, showing that their effects differ not only in magnitude but in the timescale over which they propagate — a distinction with direct implications for early warning and intervention design. Fourth, we characterize heterogeneity at a scale not previously possible: tropical populations bear the greatest burden, and access to basic infrastructure, especially electricity, substantially mitigates vulnerability, though it cannot fully protect against the damage that occurs before birth.

Methodologically, we estimate a discrete-time hazard model with rich fixed effects to isolate within-location, within-season deviations from expected climate, separately identifying positive and negative shocks using standardized temperature anomalies and the Standardized Precipitation Index. We complement the baseline linear specification with a spline design to probe threshold nonlinearities and with cumulative exposure measures to quantify sustained stress. Standard errors are clustered at the ERA5 grid cell level to account for spatial autocorrelation.

The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. Section~\ref{sec:litreview} reviews the relevant literature. Sections~\ref{sec:data} and~\ref{sec:empiricalstrategy} describe the data and empirical strategy. Section~\ref{sec:results} presents the main findings and heterogeneity analyses. Section~\ref{sec:conclusions} concludes with a discussion of policy implications.



\section{Literature Review} \label{sec:litreview}

A growing body of research has explored the intersection between climate variability and child health and survival. Prenatally and during their early years, children are biologically most vulnerable to the direct effects of weather shocks on health, nutrition, and development, including increased malnutrition, higher rates of infectious and respiratory diseases, heat stress, and elevated morbidity and mortality \parencite{caruso2024}. Climate shocks are now recognized as an increasingly prominent compounding pressure on child survival, particularly in sub-Saharan Africa and Southern Asia where baseline mortality burdens are already highest \parencite{UNIGME2025}. Understanding the causal pathways through which climate variability affects early-life survival and who bears the greatest burden, requires rigorous multi-country evidence that the existing literature has only recently begun to provide.

The most policy-relevant benchmarks for our study are the cross-country analyses that assess climate-mortality relationships across large samples over extended time periods. At the aggregate level, \textcite{burke2025} provide the most comprehensive such assessment to date, estimating that non-optimal temperatures account for 5--12 percent of all global deaths, with cold anomalies dominating heat-related mortality in temperate regions. However, their sample covers primarily high- and middle-income countries and their analysis does not address infant mortality in LMICs, where both exposure and vulnerability are substantially greater. \textcite{meierrieks2021} analyzed short-run weather shocks across 170 countries over 1960--2016, finding that a 1\textdegree C temperature increase is associated with 16.6 additional deaths per 1,000 live births — a cross-country scope that is valuable, but one that does not distinguish exposure timing within infancy or separate the roles of different climate stressors. Focusing specifically on early-life outcomes in low-income settings, \textcite{wang2025} use microdata from 33 African countries to show that in-utero exposure to extreme heat markedly increases neonatal mortality, particularly among rural and electricity-poor households. Most recently, \textcite{dimitrova2024} extend this line of work by formally attributing neonatal deaths to anthropogenic climate change, estimating — using DHS data from 29 LMICs and counterfactual temperature simulations — that roughly 4.3 percent of neonatal mortality is linked to non-optimal temperatures. These studies represent the closest analogs to our own, but both remain confined to the neonatal period and to temperature exposure alone.

Cold shocks have received comparatively less attention in this literature, despite their demonstrated importance in temperate and high-altitude settings. Pregnancy and early infancy are periods of heightened vulnerability to temperature extremes generally \parencite{strand2012,kuehn2017}, with cold exposure in particular linked to elevated risks of respiratory infections and adverse birth outcomes. \textcite{burke2025} confirm that cold dominates temperature-related mortality across temperate populations, yet this finding has not been extended to LMICs, where the existing literature has remained predominantly heat-focused. As we show, cold anomalies during gestation generate mortality increases of comparable magnitude to heat shocks across our multi-country sample — a result that the narrower neonatal literature does not anticipate.

A parallel set of single-country studies documents the health consequences of climate shocks at finer resolution, typically through nutritional or morbidity outcomes rather than mortality. Unusual weather patterns during pregnancy and early infancy can heighten the likelihood of undernutrition \parencite{dimitrova2020}, and drought exposure during fetal development has been linked to adverse health effects in early childhood \parencite{nguyen2022}. \textcite{portner2010} found significant negative 
effects of climate shocks on anthropometric outcomes in Guatemala, \textcite{borjean2012} document that rainfall shocks during the prenatal period strongly affect children's nutritional status in rural Burkina Faso, and \textcite{rabassa2014} find that low rainfall reduces weight-for-height scores in Nigeria while also pointing to diarrheal incidence as a downstream pathway. \textcite{akresh2012} further show that in-utero rainfall shocks have the largest and most persistent effects on child cognitive development, with impacts diminishing sharply for shocks experienced after birth — consistent with the fetal origins hypothesis that gestation is the most 
sensitive developmental window. This single-country evidence establishes plausible biological and economic channels, but cannot speak directly to mortality or generalize across the climatic and institutional diversity of LMICs.

On precipitation and mortality, the evidence is sparser and more mixed. \textcite{ponnusamy2022} found that negative precipitation anomalies raise child deaths across developing countries, and \textcite{han2013} document similar results for Mali, while maternal heat exposure has been consistently linked to adverse in-utero outcomes including shorter gestation, lower birthweight, and stillbirth \parencite{kuehn2017,strand2012,sun2020}. Importantly, none of these studies examine whether precipitation effects operate through short-run monthly shocks or through slower-moving cumulative processes; a distinction that, as we show, is critical for correctly interpreting 
the evidence. It is also worth noting that increases in average rainfall can be beneficial: in Indonesia, \textcite{maccini2008} found that higher early-life rainfall has large positive effects on the long-run health, education, and socioeconomic outcomes of women — most plausibly through higher agricultural income and food availability — underscoring that not all climatic variability is detrimental.

Relative to this literature, our study makes four contributions. We extend the analysis of \textcite{dimitrova2024} and \textcite{wang2025} beyond the neonatal period, tracing temperature effects from gestation through the first two years of life across sixty countries. We show that mortality responds to sustained anomalies rather than isolated extremes, and that temperature and precipitation differ fundamentally in the timescale over which their effects propagate. We further characterize 
heterogeneity across climatic zones and household infrastructure at a scale not feasible within single-country designs. Together these contributions provide the most comprehensive evidence to date on how climate variability shapes infant survival in the world's most vulnerable populations.


\section{Data} \label{sec:data}

This study combines individual-level microdata from the Demographic and Health Surveys (DHS) with high-resolution climate reanalysis data from ERA5. The DHS provide geo-referenced birth histories and household characteristics across low- and middle-income countries; ERA5 supplies monthly temperature and precipitation data at global coverage. Linking the two at the cluster level and the monthly frequency produces the individual-level exposure records that underpin our empirical strategy.

\subsection{Demographic and Health Surveys} \label{sec:data_DHS}

The DHS program conducts nationally representative household surveys in low- and middle-income countries, collecting detailed information on fertility, child health, and household living conditions. We draw on surveys from 60 countries covering the period 1998--2021, yielding a harmonized panel of over four million births. Figure~\ref{fig:distr_DHS} maps the geographic coverage of the surveys included in the analysis.

All DHS surveys collect complete birth histories from women aged 15--49, recording the date of birth and survival status of each live-born child. For children who died, age at death is recorded in days (if under 28 days), months (if under two years), or years. We exploit this structure to define mortality outcomes across five discrete windows: the neonatal period ($\leq$30 days), and the 1--6, 6--12, 12--18, and 18--24 month intervals. Figure~\ref{fig:deaths_distr} shows the distribution of deaths across these windows in our sample. Mortality is heavily concentrated in the first six months of life — roughly 44 deaths per 1,000 live births — declining sharply thereafter, consistent with declining biological vulnerability as children age.\footnote{A well-documented feature of DHS birth histories is age heaping at 12 months, arising from the rounding of deaths initially reported in years to an exact month threshold. We follow standard practice by defining infant mortality as death within 12 completed months, and verify robustness by excluding observations with imprecise age-at-death reporting in sensitivity checks \parencite{gakidou2006}.} We restrict the sample to births occurring within ten years prior to the interview date to ensure temporal accuracy and reduce the risk of recall error.

We link each child's mortality record to climate data using the GPS coordinates of the DHS cluster where the mother resides at the time of interview. This approximation is standard in the literature \parencite{wang2025,dimitrova2024}, but for births that occurred years before the interview, the mother's current location may differ from her location at the time of birth or gestation. To the extent that residential mobility is uncorrelated with climate conditions, this introduces classical measurement error in the exposure variable that will tend to attenuate our estimates toward zero, implying that our results are likely conservative rather than inflated.\footnote{The DHS collects information on duration of residence at the current location (\texttt{v103}--\texttt{v104}), which would allow a direct robustness check restricted to non-mobile mothers. We flag this as a robustness exercise for future work.} To further improve spatial consistency, we restrict the sample to surveys conducted from 2003 onward, when DHS began applying a standardized spatial displacement protocol that randomly offsets urban cluster coordinates by up to 2 km and rural coordinates by up to 5 km (with 1\% displaced up to 10 km). Surveys conducted prior to 2003 relied on less precise geolocation methods that could introduce larger spatial measurement error when linking to gridded climate data.

Beyond mortality outcomes, we construct a set of child, maternal, and household variables from the DHS birth and household survey files, which serve as controls in the main specification and as dimensions of the heterogeneity analysis. These include child sex, multiple-birth status, and birth order; maternal age at birth and years of completed education; household electricity access, source of drinking water, the DHS wealth index \parencite{filmer2001}, and a cold-weather protection proxy based on housing material quality. Full details of variable construction, DHS source codes, and the role of each variable in the analysis are provided in Appendix Table~\ref{tab:dhs_variables}, and descriptive statistics for the main control variables are reported in Appendix Table~\ref{tab:descr}.\footnote{For cross-country comparability, we developed a modular cleaning pipeline in Stata. A master script processes the birth, household, and GPS files for each country survey, harmonizing variable formats, merging GPS coordinates, converting non-Gregorian calendars (e.g.\ the Ethiopian calendar), and aligning administrative identifiers across survey rounds. Full details are provided in Appendix~\ref{app:dhs}.}

\subsection{Climate Data} \label{sec:data_climate}

We use ERA5, produced by the European Centre for Medium-Range Weather Forecasts (ECMWF), as our primary climate data source. ERA5 is a state-of-the-art global reanalysis combining historical observations with a numerical weather model to produce hourly estimates of atmospheric variables from 1940 to present on a 0.5\textdegree{} $\times$ 0.5\textdegree{} grid (approximately 50 km $\times$ 50 km at the equator) \parencite{hersbach2020}. We use monthly aggregates of average temperature and accumulated precipitation, from which we construct two standardized shock measures.

For temperature, we compute for each ERA5 grid cell $c$ and calendar month $m$ the 30-year mean $\overline{t}_{cm}$ and standard deviation $\sigma_{cm}$ over the baseline period 1969--1998.\footnote{We use 1969--1998 as the baseline rather than a more recent period to avoid conflating the anomaly measure with anthropogenic warming trends. Using a contemporaneous distribution would compress anomalies over time as the climate warms, mechanically attenuating our estimates. Appendix~\ref{app:robustness} shows that results are robust to alternative baseline periods.} The standardized anomaly for month $m$ in year $y$ is:
%
\[ T_{cym} = \frac{t_{cym} - \overline{t}_{cm}}{\sigma_{cm}} \]
%
where $t_{cym}$ is the observed monthly mean temperature. A value of $T_{cym} = 1$ means the month was one standard deviation warmer than historically typical for that location and season. Standardizing by the local historical distribution serves two purposes. First, it makes anomalies comparable across climatically diverse locations: a 2\textdegree C departure carries different biological meaning in a 34\textdegree C tropical environment than in a 24\textdegree C temperate one. Second, it eliminates seasonal patterns by construction, isolating deviations from what households in each location have historically experienced in that specific month of the year.

For precipitation, we use the Standardized Precipitation Index (SPI) \parencite{mckee1993}, a widely used index that expresses precipitation anomalies relative to the long-run historical distribution. The SPI is computed by fitting a gamma distribution to the long-term precipitation record for each grid cell and calendar month, then transforming the resulting cumulative probabilities to a standard normal scale. An SPI of zero indicates median conditions; positive values indicate wetter-than-median conditions; negative values indicate drier-than-median conditions. Our primary measure is the one-month SPI (SPI-1), which captures short-run precipitation anomalies. We also compute multi-month SPIs (SPI-3, SPI-6, SPI-9, SPI-12, and SPI-24) for the cumulative exposure analyses in Section~\ref{sec:results_cumulative}.

For both temperature and precipitation, we create separate variables to capture anomalies in each direction. For temperature, we define $T^+_{cym} = \max(T_{cym}, 0)$ and $T^-_{cym} = \max(-T_{cym}, 0)$, so that $T^+$ captures heat shocks and $T^-$ captures cold shocks, each set to zero in the opposite case. We apply the same decomposition to the SPI: $P^+_{cym} = \max(\text{SPI1}_{cym}, 0)$ captures excess rainfall and $P^-_{cym} = \max(-\text{SPI1}_{cym}, 0)$ captures deficits. This allows us to estimate asymmetric effects of heat versus cold and of flood versus drought without imposing symmetry on the dose-response relationship.

\subsection{Linking Climate Data to Children} \label{sec:data_link}

We link each child's birth record to monthly climate data using the GPS coordinates of the DHS cluster and the reconstructed birth date. For each child, we extract monthly temperature and SPI values covering the nine months of gestation and the first 36 months of life, giving a complete climate exposure history from conception through early childhood. These monthly values are then aggregated into exposure windows that balance temporal granularity against statistical power: in utero (nine-month average), 0--6 months, 6--12 months, 12--18 months, 18--24 months, 24--30 months, and 30--36 months. Using six-month postnatal windows allows direct comparison of effects across childhood stages while preserving sufficient observations in each cell for precise estimation.

The in-utero window differs from the postnatal windows in one important respect: averaging over nine months rather than six compresses the distribution of the resulting indicator. As Figure~\ref{fig:distributions} shows, the in-utero shock distributions have narrower tails than the postnatal distributions, a one-standard-deviation in-utero anomaly therefore represents a more sustained and climatologically unusual departure from normal conditions than the same standardized deviation computed over a shorter postnatal window.\footnote{Because the variance of anomalies decreases as the averaging window grows, a one-standard-deviation shock measured over nine months is a rarer event than a one-standard-deviation shock measured over six months. This implies that in-utero and postnatal coefficients are not directly commensurable in magnitude; we return to this point in Section~\ref{sec:results_main}.}

\section{Empirical Identification Strategy} \label{sec:empiricalstrategy}

Our empirical strategy aims to isolate the causal impact of climate shocks on infant mortality by exploiting within-location, within-season variation in temperature and precipitation. We estimate a discrete-time hazard model, focusing on the probability of death in each period of infancy conditional on survival to the previous period. This framework is well-suited for analyzing infant mortality, which is heavily concentrated in the first months of life and declines rapidly with age.

Because both temperature and precipitation measures are standardized, deviations further from zero — whether positive or negative — are expected to have a larger impact. To maintain parsimony and ease of interpretation, our primary specification models linear effects of these deviations, enabling us to separately estimate the effects of high and low temperature and precipitation shocks. Our baseline regression model is:

\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
D_{icym}^p = \alpha &+ \sum_{\rho} \left( P^+_{\rho cym}\beta^{P^+}_{\rho} + P^-_{\rho cym}\beta^{P^-}_{\rho} + T^+_{\rho cym}\beta^{T^+}_{\rho} + T^-_{\rho cym}\beta^{T^-}_{\rho} \right) \\
&+ \theta X_{icym} + \delta_{cm} + \gamma_{c}y + \varepsilon_{icym}
\end{split}
\label{originalmodel}
\end{equation}

where $D_{icym}^p$ is a dummy variable equal to 1 if child $i$, living in cell $c$, born in year $y$ and month $m$, died in period $p$ (multiplied by 1,000 to express mortality rates per 1,000 live births). $T^+_{\rho cym}$ and $T^-_{\rho cym}$ are the positive and negative components of the standardized temperature anomaly in cell $c$ during exposure window $\rho$, constructed as described in Section~\ref{sec:data_climate}; $P^+_{\rho cym}$ and $P^-_{\rho cym}$ are the analogous precipitation variables. The index $\rho$ runs over the in-utero period and the subsequent 6-month postnatal windows (0--6, 6--12, 12--18, 18--24, 24--30, and 30--36 months). $X_{icym}$ is a vector of individual and household controls including child sex, multiple-birth indicator, birth order, maternal age (cubic), maternal education (cubic), rural residence indicator, and wealth index quintile dummies. $\delta_{cm}$ denotes cell-month fixed effects and $\gamma_{c}y$ denotes cell-specific linear time trends.\footnote{[NICO TO CONFIRM: the sum over $\rho$ should include the in-utero period — please verify the lower bound of the sum matches the actual do-file specification.]} To interpret the coefficients: a coefficient of 1.5 on the in-utero heat shock ($\beta^{T^+}_{\text{utero}}$) implies that a one-standard-deviation above-normal temperature during gestation is associated with 1.5 additional infant deaths per 1,000 live births in the relevant mortality window. [NICO TO CONFIRM: adjust this number to match the actual estimate.]

Cell-month fixed effects control for time-invariant characteristics within each ERA5 grid cell specific to each calendar month — including baseline climatic conditions, seasonal disease patterns, and agricultural cycles — absorbing any common seasonal variation in mortality. Cell-specific linear time trends allow for spatially heterogeneous long-run improvements or deteriorations in infant health that might be correlated with climate change, ensuring that the estimated coefficients reflect deviations from local trends rather than slow-moving confounders. Standard errors are clustered at the ERA5 grid cell level to account for spatial autocorrelation and serial correlation within cells.

To probe non-linearities in the dose-response relationship, we also estimate a spline specification that allows the mortality response to differ across the distribution of shock magnitudes. For each climate variable and exposure period $\rho$, we define four categories based on the magnitude of the anomaly: large negative ($C_1: \leq -1$ SD), moderate negative ($C_2: -1$ to $0$ SD), moderate positive ($C_3: 0$ to $+1$ SD), and large positive ($C_4: \geq +1$ SD). The $\pm 1$ SD thresholds are computed from the full DHS sample distribution and correspond to the natural boundary between near-normal and truly anomalous conditions in a standardized normal distribution, consistent with the cutoffs visible in Figure~\ref{fig:distributions}. The spline model is:

\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
D_{icym}^p = \alpha &+ \sum_{\rho} \sum_{k=1}^{4} \Bigl( \beta^{P}_{k\rho} I(P_{\rho cym} \in C_k) P_{\rho cym} + \beta^{T}_{k\rho} I(T_{\rho cym} \in C_k) T_{\rho cym} \Bigr) \\
&+ \theta X_{icym} + \delta_{cm} + \gamma_{cy} + \varepsilon_{icym}
\end{split}
\label{splinemodel}
\end{equation}

where $I(T_{\rho cym} \in C_k)$ is an indicator equal to 1 if the temperature anomaly in period $\rho$ falls within category $C_k$, and analogously for precipitation. The coefficient $\beta^{T}_{k\rho}$ captures the slope of the mortality-temperature relationship within category $k$ during window $\rho$, allowing us to test whether the mortality response is approximately linear across the shock distribution or whether it is concentrated among truly extreme anomalies. All other notation is as in Equation~\ref{originalmodel}.

We investigate heterogeneity in climate shock impacts along two dimensions that shape both exposure and adaptive capacity. First, we classify DHS clusters into broad climate zones — tropical, arid, and temperate — using the Köppen-Geiger classification \parencite{kottek2006,rubel2010}, whose geographic distribution is shown in Figure~\ref{fig:distr_KG}, and estimate separate regressions for each zone. Second, we stratify countries by World Bank income group (low-income, lower-middle-income, and upper-middle-income), shown in Figure~\ref{fig:distr_wbincomegroup}, to assess whether vulnerability differs with the level of economic development and public health infrastructure. We additionally examine heterogeneity at the household level by splitting the sample by electricity access and cold-weather protection, as these dimensions of infrastructure mediate the capacity to buffer against temperature extremes.

\section{Results} \label{sec:results}

We present the results in four steps. We begin with the baseline effects of temperature and precipitation anomalies on infant mortality across gestation and early childhood. We then examine the cumulative nature of climate stress, comparing sustained monthly anomalies against isolated extreme events, tracing how effects build as exposure windows lengthen, and assessing whether in-utero shocks leave a lasting imprint on survival beyond the neonatal period. We translate the micro estimates into aggregate terms, mapping the spatial concentration and temporal dynamics of climate-attributable infant deaths across the DHS sample. We close by characterizing heterogeneity across climatic zones and household infrastructure.

Across all specifications, four findings emerge consistently. Temperature anomalies substantially raise infant mortality, with gestational exposure producing the largest and most persistent effects, risks remain elevated well into the first year of life even when postnatal conditions are normal. These effects are driven 
by sustained departures from expected climate conditions rather than isolated extreme events. Precipitation behaves differently: monthly rainfall anomalies have little detectable effect, but sustained wet conditions over many months do raise mortality, consistent with slower-moving contamination and disease channels. Finally, the burden 
is deeply unequal — tropical regions, infrastructure-poor households, and communities in Sub-Saharan Africa and South Asia bear a disproportionate share of climate-attributable infant deaths.

\subsection{Main Effects of Climate Shocks} \label{sec:results_main}

\subsubsection{Temperature Anomalies: Timing and Persistence}

Figure~\ref{fig:temp_shocks} presents the estimated effects of one-standard-deviation temperature anomalies on infant mortality, distinguishing between unusually high and unusually low temperatures. Each line corresponds to the timing of the shock (in utero, 0--6 months, 6--12 months, etc.), while the y-axis reflects the change in deaths per 1,000 live births. Shaded areas denote 95 percent confidence intervals.

Three main findings emerge. First, in-utero exposure produces the largest increases in infant mortality in absolute terms. A one-standard-deviation (SD) high-temperature shock during gestation raises mortality in the first six months of life by about 1.5 deaths per 1,000 births, roughly a 3--4 percent increase relative to the 
baseline neonatal mortality rate of approximately 44 deaths per 1,000 live births in our sample. Cold anomalies of similar magnitude increase mortality by roughly 1.0--1.2 deaths per 1,000.These effects are statistically significant and larger in absolute magnitude than those associated with exposures at later ages, underscoring that the prenatal period is the most vulnerable. 

Second, postnatal shocks also raise mortality, though more modestly in absolute terms and for shorter durations. A 1-SD high-temperature anomaly during the 6--12 month period increases mortality by about 1.1 deaths per 1,000 — a substantially larger proportional effect of roughly 14 percent relative to the baseline of approximately 8 deaths per 1,000 in that window. This contrast is worth noting: although in-utero shocks are larger in absolute terms, postnatal shocks hit a much lower baseline, implying that their proportional toll on surviving children is particularly severe. The effect of postnatal shocks fades relatively quickly and is not consistently detectable beyond the subsequent semester; by the second year of life, most postnatal estimates are small and statistically indistinguishable from zero, consistent with increasing resilience as children age.

Third, the effects are broadly symmetric: both unusually hot and unusually cold anomalies compromise survival. Although confidence intervals are wider for cold shocks, particularly after six months of age, the pattern indicates that deviations in either direction from expected climatic conditions pose substantial risks to infant health. These results align with evidence from sub-Saharan Africa showing that in-utero exposure to extreme heat significantly increases neonatal mortality \parencite{wang2025}, and with cross-country evidence attributing a measurable share of neonatal deaths in LMICs to non-optimal temperatures \parencite{dimitrova2024}. Our findings extend these relationships globally and beyond the neonatal period, revealing that temperature anomalies during gestation raise mortality risks well into the first year of life.

Whether these mortality effects reflect acute responses to isolated extreme events or the accumulation of sustained climate stress is the subject of Section~\ref{sec:results_cumulative}.

\subsubsection{Precipitation Anomalies: Monthly Effects}

Having established that temperature anomalies are immediate and persistent predictors of infant mortality, we next examine precipitation anomalies using the same baseline specification. The contrast between the two stressors is striking — and informative about the pathways through which climate variability damages early-life survival.

Temperature imposes physiological stress directly and rapidly: heat accelerates dehydration and febrile illness, cold elevates respiratory infection risk, and both compromise fetal development when sustained in utero. Monthly anomalies are therefore sufficient to detect mortality effects. Precipitation, by contrast, does not act on infant health through immediate biological pathways. Its effects travel through slower-moving channels — contamination of water sources, proliferation of disease vectors, disruption of agricultural output, and flooding-related deterioration of living conditions — each of which requires weeks to months to translate into elevated mortality. The implication is that a monthly precipitation shock should have little detectable effect on infant mortality, not because rainfall is irrelevant to child health, but because the relevant unit of exposure is cumulative rather than contemporaneous.

A few suggestive cases emerge — a high-precipitation shock during gestation is associated with a small, marginally significant increase in mortality — but these patterns are not robust across periods or specifications. \subsection{The Cumulative Nature of Climate Shocks} \label{sec:results_cumulative}

The baseline results establish that temperature anomalies raise infant mortality, with effects concentrated in the prenatal period and fading over the first year of life. Yet these estimates, based on six-month exposure windows, may understate the full burden of climate stress if its effects build gradually over time. This section examines three complementary dimensions of cumulative exposure: whether mortality responds more to sustained monthly anomalies or to isolated extreme days; how estimated effects evolve as exposure windows lengthen from one to nine months; and whether gestational shocks leave a lasting imprint on survival that persists well after birth.

\subsubsection{Cumulative Anomalies vs. Extreme Days}

As a first step, we compare two alternative measures of temperature exposure: monthly anomalies, which capture sustained departures from normal conditions, and counts of extreme hot or cold days within an otherwise average month. This horserace analysis directly tests whether infant mortality responds to persistent climate stress or to isolated acute events.

Figure~\ref{fig:temperature_horserace} presents the results. Each panel corresponds to the timing of exposure (in utero, 0--6 months, 6--12 months, etc.), with solid orange and blue dots representing high- and low-temperature anomalies, and lighter markers denoting the number of days above 35\textdegree C or below 0\textdegree C. Two patterns stand out. First, mortality risk is closely tied to sustained anomalies rather than isolated extreme days: coefficients on monthly temperature anomalies are consistently larger and more often statistically significant, while those for extreme-day counts remain close to zero across all exposure windows. Second, the contrast is quantitatively striking — a month that is consistently warmer than usual imposes a far greater mortality cost than a month with a few exceptionally hot days embedded in otherwise normal conditions. What kills infants is not the heatwave per se, but the sustained departure from expected climate conditions.

\subsubsection{Length of Exposure Windows}

We next trace how mortality effects evolve as exposure windows lengthen. Figure~\ref{fig:cumulative_shocks} plots the estimated effect of one-standard-deviation anomalies averaged over windows from one to nine months, covering the full prenatal period and the neonatal month. The outcome is first-month mortality. If climate effects were driven by acute events, extending the averaging window would dilute their signal and attenuate the coefficients. If instead mortality responds to sustained exposure, coefficients should grow as the window lengthens.

The evidence strongly favors the sustained-exposure interpretation. For high temperatures (top left panel), the coefficient is close to zero when averaged over a single month but rises steadily with longer windows, reaching approximately 0.7--0.8 excess deaths per 1,000 births at nine months. The pattern is even more pronounced for cold anomalies (top right): effects increase consistently, reaching roughly 1.2--1.3 excess deaths per 1,000 at nine months.\footnote{Because the variance of anomalies decreases as the averaging window grows, a 1-SD deviation over nine months represents a rarer and more persistent event than a 1-SD deviation over one month. The growing coefficient therefore reflects both a longer duration of exposure and the greater rarity of sustained anomalies.} Precipitation tells a complementary story: short-term rainfall deviations remain insignificant across all windows (consistent with Appendix Figure~\ref{fig:precip_shocks}), but high precipitation averaged over eight to nine months is associated with roughly one additional infant death per 1,000 births (bottom left). Cumulative drought (bottom right) remains largely insignificant even at longer windows, suggesting that its effects on infant mortality — likely operating through maternal nutrition and food insecurity — may manifest on timescales that extend beyond the first year of life.

\subsubsection{Persistence of In-Utero Exposure}

Finally, we assess whether gestational shocks leave a lasting imprint on child survival beyond the period of exposure itself. Figure~\ref{fig:in_utero_shocks} compares children gestated under extreme temperatures with those who were not, tracing mortality effects at successive ages. Colored lines represent the timing of the shock, with shaded bands showing 95 percent confidence intervals.

Two patterns stand out. For children exposed to extreme temperatures in utero, both heat (top left) and cold (bottom left), mortality remains elevated by 2--3 deaths per 1,000 births throughout the first six months of life, with detectable effects persisting through the second year even when postnatal climate conditions are normal. This scarring pattern is absent among children not exposed to extreme gestational conditions (right panels), where postnatal shocks generate only modest and short-lived effects. The persistence of in-utero impacts well beyond the neonatal period, and their independence from subsequent postnatal conditions, establishes gestation as the critical window of vulnerability in the climate-mortality relationship.

Taken together, the results in this section point consistently to one conclusion: infant mortality is driven not by acute weather extremes but by sustained climate stress accumulated over critical developmental windows, with the gestational period emerging as the most consequential. The following section translates these estimates into aggregate terms to assess the spatial and temporal distribution of the implied mortality burden.



\subsection{The Scale and Distribution of Climate-Attributable Mortality} \label{sec:results_attributable}

Having quantified the direct and cumulative effects of temperature anomalies on infant survival, we now translate these estimates into aggregate terms — assessing where climate-related infant deaths are concentrated and how large the implied mortality burden is across space and time.

\subsubsection{Spatial Distribution}

Figure~\ref{fig:map_deaths} translates our regression estimates into a map of infant deaths attributable to temperature anomalies. To construct it, we compute for each grid cell the average magnitude of positive and negative temperature anomalies over the sample period, multiply these by the corresponding statistically significant coefficients from the main specification — summing the estimated effects for in-utero, 0--6 month, and 6--12 month exposures — and aggregate the contributions of heat and cold shocks to obtain the estimated number of climate-attributable infant deaths per 1,000 live births. The map therefore captures both the frequency and the intensity of local climate shocks, weighted by their estimated mortality consequences.

The results reveal a pronounced geographic concentration in tropical regions of Sub-Saharan Africa, South Asia, and parts of Central and South America. In the most affected areas, temperature anomalies are associated with up to 4.5 additional infant deaths per 1,000 live births. These regions coincide with some of the poorest and most resource-constrained settings in the DHS sample, and with some of the world's highest baseline infant mortality rates \parencite{WHO2022,UNICEF2023}.\footnote{[NICO TO COMPLETE: In our DHS sample, the average infant mortality rate is XX per 1,000 in Sub-Saharan Africa, YY per 1,000 in South Asia, and ZZ per 1,000 in Latin America.]} The geographic pattern closely mirrors the UNICEF IGME (2025) assessment of where the global child mortality burden falls today \parencite{UNIGME2025}, and underscores that climate variability both threatens infant survival and compounds existing global health inequalities.

\subsection{Nonlinear Effects}

The baseline specification assumes that the mortality response to climate anomalies is linear — that a two-standard-deviation shock imposes exactly twice the mortality cost of a one-standard-deviation shock. We now relax this assumption to assess whether the dose-response relationship exhibits threshold behavior, and whether the linear model provides a reasonable approximation across the range of observed shocks.

Following Equation~\ref{splinemodel}, we estimate separate slope coefficients for four categories of shock magnitude — large negative ($\leq -1$ SD), moderate negative ($-1$ to $0$ SD), moderate positive ($0$ to $+1$ SD), and large positive ($\geq +1$ SD) — allowing the mortality response to differ across the distribution of climate anomalies. Given statistical power constraints, we focus on mortality in the first six months of life, where effects are largest and the number of deaths is sufficient to detect differences across categories.

Figure~\ref{fig:spline_coefs_temp} presents the results for temperature. The pattern is consistent across both exposure windows: it is the extreme categories — large heat and large cold shocks ($\geq +1$ SD and $\leq -1$ SD) — that drive the mortality effects documented in the baseline specification, while moderate anomalies produce coefficients that are imprecisely estimated and statistically indistinguishable from zero. This threshold-type pattern holds for both in-utero and postnatal (0--6 month) exposure, suggesting that the mortality response is not smoothly proportional to shock magnitude but is instead concentrated among truly anomalous climate conditions. Infants and fetuses appear largely resilient to moderate deviations from expected temperatures, but face sharply elevated mortality risks once anomalies cross into extreme territory.

The implications for the linear specification are twofold. The linear model correctly captures the direction and approximate magnitude of the average effect across the full distribution of shocks. However, it implicitly attributes mortality costs to moderate anomalies that may in practice be negligible, while accurately capturing the toll from extreme events. Aggregate burden calculations based on the linear coefficients should therefore be interpreted as reflecting primarily the mortality cost of truly extreme climate anomalies, with the contribution of near-average deviations likely overstated.

Figure~\ref{fig:spline_coefs_spi} confirms that the precipitation null result documented in Section~\ref{sec:results_main} is not an artifact of the linear specification. Across all four shock categories and both exposure windows, coefficients on precipitation anomalies remain close to zero and statistically insignificant, including for the most extreme wet and dry conditions, reinforcing that short-run monthly rainfall deviations do not generate detectable mortality effects regardless of their magnitude. The full set of spline coefficients across all exposure windows is reported in Appendix~\ref{app:additional_results}.

\subsection{Heterogeneity in Impacts} \label{sec:results_heterogeneity}

Figure~\ref{fig:climate_bands} illustrates how the effects of temperature anomalies on infant mortality differ across major climate zones. Tropical regions show the clearest and most persistent effects: both heat and cold anomalies in utero raise mortality by roughly 2-3 deaths per 1,000 births in the first six months of life, with elevated risks that decay gradually but remain detectable through the second year. In tropical settings the in-utero exposure line consistently dominates postnatal shocks, reinforcing that gestation is the window of greatest vulnerability regardless of the direction of the anomaly.

In temperate regions, the pattern shifts. Heat anomalies remain detectable but are smaller and estimated with greater uncertainty. Cold anomalies, however, produce an in-utero coefficient comparable in magnitude to the tropical estimates, consistent with the broader literature showing that cold dominates temperature-related mortality in non-tropical climates \parencite{burke2025}. The arid zone results are the most difficult to interpret: point estimates for in-utero cold exposure are among the highest in the figure, but confidence intervals are wide, reflecting both the smaller number of DHS clusters in arid regions and greater within-category heterogeneity. Heat effects in arid settings appear muted. These results should be interpreted with caution given the imprecision of the estimates.

Turning to household-level heterogeneity, Figure~\ref{fig:electricity} shows that access to electricity substantially mitigates the mortality impacts of temperature shocks, though it does not eliminate them entirely. Among households without electricity, both heat and cold anomalies raise infant mortality by 2--3 deaths per 1,000 births in the first six months, with effects that remain elevated through 18--24 months, the most persistent pattern observed across any subgroup in our analysis. Among households with electricity access, postnatal shocks are largely attenuated and often statistically indistinguishable from zero. However, in-utero exposure retains a detectable effect even among electrified households, with cold shocks in utero still generating roughly 1.5--2.0 additional deaths per 1,000 births. Electricity therefore protects against postnatal climate stress more effectively than against gestational exposure, a distinction with direct implications for the design of adaptive interventions, which we take up in the Discussion.

\section{Discussion} \label{sec:discussion}

This paper set out to answer three questions left open by the existing literature: how climate effects on infant survival evolve beyond the neonatal period, whether mortality responds to acute extremes or sustained anomalies, and how temperature and precipitation compare as mortality drivers. The results provide consistent answers to all three. Temperature anomalies generate large, persistent mortality effects concentrated in the gestational period; these effects are driven by sustained climate stress rather than isolated extreme events; and precipitation operates through fundamentally different, slower-moving channels that only become detectable over multi-month cumulative windows. This section situates these findings in the broader literature, discusses the mechanisms that underpin them, and draws out their implications for policy.

The patterns we document are consistent with well-established physiological pathways. Prenatal heat stress can shorten gestation, reduce birthweight, and increase neonatal vulnerability \parencite{strand2012,kuehn2017,sun2020}, leaving infants more susceptible to infections and nutritional deficits throughout the first year of life. Cold anomalies during gestation likely elevate risks through complementary pathways, including increased susceptibility to respiratory infections and physiological stress on placental function, though the precise mechanisms remain less studied than those for heat. Postnatal exposures appear to operate through more immediate channels: high temperatures are associated with dehydration and febrile illness, while cold shocks tend to exacerbate respiratory conditions. The finding that mortality responds to sustained monthly anomalies rather than isolated extreme days is consistent with this interpretation. It is the continuous physiological strain, rather than a single acute event, that plausibly accumulates into elevated mortality risk.

The contrast between temperature and precipitation reflects fundamentally different transmission pathways. Unlike temperature, which imposes direct physiological stress on fetuses and infants, precipitation affects child health through slower-moving channels: contamination of water sources, proliferation of disease vectors, disruption of agricultural output, and flooding-related deterioration of living conditions, each of which requires weeks to months to translate into elevated mortality. This explains why monthly rainfall anomalies are uninformative but sustained wet conditions over eight to nine months generate detectable effects. The asymmetry between cumulative high precipitation and cumulative drought also reflects channel differences. Flooding-related contamination accumulates relatively quickly once conditions deteriorate, whereas drought operates primarily through maternal nutritional depletion and food insecurity, processes that unfold slowly and whose consequences for infant survival may extend beyond the first year of life, manifesting as reduced birthweight or stunting that predisposes children to later mortality.

The geographic concentration of climate-attributable mortality in tropical sub-Saharan Africa and South Asia reflects the intersection of high climate exposure, high baseline vulnerability, and limited adaptive capacity. Our finding that heat anomalies dominate in tropical settings while cold anomalies are comparably important in temperate zones mirrors the adult mortality evidence from \textcite{burke2025} and suggests that infants face the same climatological gradient as the general population, amplified by their heightened biological sensitivity. The muted heat effects in arid zones, where point estimates are large but imprecise, may reflect long-standing behavioral and physiological adaptation to high baseline temperatures, or distinct disease ecologies in which the pathways linking heat to infant mortality are less active. These geographic patterns are consistent with the most recent global child mortality assessments, which identify the same regions as bearing the heaviest burden of preventable child deaths and facing the greatest compounding pressure from climate change \parencite{UNIGME2025}.

Our heterogeneity results carry a nuanced message about the role of infrastructure in moderating climate vulnerability. Access to electricity substantially reduces the mortality impacts of postnatal temperature shocks, consistent with \textcite{wang2025} for sub-Saharan Africa and with \textcite{burke2025} for the broader cross-country evidence on cooling and infrastructure. However, electrification does not eliminate in-utero effects: even among electrified households, cold shocks during gestation generate roughly 1.5--2.0 additional deaths per 1,000 births. Electricity can moderate the thermal environment of a home and preserve food and medicines, protecting infants from postnatal heat and cold stress. But it cannot easily address the physiological processes through which gestational temperature exposure damages fetal development, processes mediated by maternal health, placental function, and prenatal care rather than by household cooling capacity. The implication is that infrastructure investment, while necessary, is not sufficient [For Jed: Not sure how to address the need for infrastructure but also mentioning this can be a problem as well, a loop, add any ideas]. Reducing the gestational mortality burden will additionally require interventions that directly protect maternal health during pregnancy: access to prenatal care, targeted support for pregnant women during climate extremes, and early warning systems that trigger protective responses before the most vulnerable developmental windows are compromised.

The scale of the implied mortality burden, approximately 44 million infant deaths attributable to temperature variability across DHS countries between 1992 and 2022, underscores that climate change is not a future health threat but a present driver of mortality in the world's most vulnerable populations. The finding that sustained anomalies drive mortality rather than isolated extreme events suggests that adaptation strategies focused narrowly on heatwave response may miss a substantial share of the burden. Early warning systems that respond to prolonged departures from normal conditions, rather than just acute extremes, are likely to be more effective. The differential behavior of temperature and precipitation further implies that these stressors require different monitoring timescales: temperature effects manifest quickly and require rapid response, while precipitation effects accumulate slowly and call for longer-horizon surveillance of cumulative rainfall conditions. More broadly, the geographic concentration of burden in tropical regions with low adaptive capacity points to a fundamental equity dimension. The populations most exposed to climate-related infant mortality are also those with the fewest resources to adapt, reinforcing the case for international support for adaptation in low-income tropical settings.

Several limitations of this study warrant acknowledgment. We approximate each child's location of birth using the mother's current DHS cluster, which may introduce classical measurement error that attenuates our estimates toward zero [pg comment: DEPENDING ON NEW RESULTS USING LOCATION WE MIGHT MODIFY THIS]. The DHS microdata do not allow us to directly observe the biological mechanisms linking climate exposure to mortality, so the channel evidence is inferential rather than direct. The aggregate burden calculations rest on the assumption that the spatial distribution of infants tracks the general population, which may introduce geographic imprecision. And the 30-year sample window, while long by microeconomic standards, is too short to identify secular trends in climate-mortality relationships with confidence. \section{Detailed DHS Data Processing} \label{app:dhs}

To prepare the DHS data for linkage with climate information, we developed a modular cleaning pipeline in Stata. A single master script manages the process for each country, sequentially loading, harmonizing, and processing the raw birth, household, and GPS datasets. The pipeline proceeds through the following steps:  

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Country-Level Data Assembly.}  
    For each survey, we import the raw DHS birth and household files along with the corresponding GPS cluster file. File names and variable formats are standardized across countries and survey rounds, with special handling for countries (e.g., Liberia, Malawi) that employ distinct survey templates or coding conventions.  

    \item \textbf{Variable Harmonization and Cleaning.}  
    A modular set of cleaning scripts harmonizes key maternal, child, and household variables across surveys, including age, education, assets, birth timing, and child survival status. Variables not consistently available or not relevant to the analysis are excluded using curated country-specific keep-lists.  

    \item \textbf{Spatial Merging and Conversion.}  
    Cluster-level GPS coordinates are merged into the birth datasets using unique identifiers. Coordinates are converted from degrees--minutes--seconds into decimal degrees to ensure compatibility with gridded climate data. The DHS displacement protocol (up to 2 km in urban areas, 5 km in rural areas, and 10 km for 1\% of clusters) is explicitly accounted for during spatial processing.  

    \item \textbf{Temporal and Geographic Standardization.}  
    Survey-specific calendars (e.g., the Ethiopian calendar) are converted to Gregorian dates. Date-of-birth and age-at-death variables are standardized, and administrative regions are harmonized across rounds to ensure consistent geographic identifiers.  

    \item \textbf{Saving Cleaned Data.}  
    The cleaned datasets are saved at the country level with harmonized variable names, formats, and identifiers. These country-level files serve as the foundation for pooled analysis and for constructing individual-level measures of exposure to climate variability.  
\end{enumerate}

This process produced a harmonized panel of over four million births with consistent measures of mortality, household characteristics, location, and timing.
